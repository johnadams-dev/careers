How many licensed real estate professionals are there?

Counted from Florida's weekly license file on September 22, 2026, Seminole County holds 4,254 active sales associates, 1,058 active brokers and 407 broker associates. They work through 697 registered brokerage firms and 161 branch offices.

To put that in proportion, Seminole has nearly as many working agents as Volusia County next door, in roughly half the land area.

The number that says most about this market

2,711 Seminole County sales associate licenses are inactive. That is 38.9% of every sales associate license issued to the county, against 32.4% statewide, and it is the highest rate of any sizable county in Florida.

An inactive license is not expired and not revoked. It belongs to someone who completed the 63-hour course, passed the state exam, paid the fees, and is not currently registered under an employing broker. A rate that high, in a prosperous suburban county with an active housing market, is not explained by the market being bad. It is a statement about how many people start this career and do not sustain it.

Read the other way, it is also the size of the pool. 2,711 people in this county already hold a license and are not using it.

How the county's agents are distributed

By the address each licensee has on file with the state:

  • Longwood: 931 active licensees
  • Oviedo: 853 active licensees
  • Sanford: 800 active licensees
  • Lake Mary: 746 active licensees
  • Altamonte Springs: 606 active licensees
  • Winter Springs: 570 active licensees
  • Casselberry: 314 active licensees

No single city dominates, which is unusual and worth noting. Longwood holds the most and it is under a quarter of the county. An agent here works a territory rather than a town.

How crowded is it, and who do you work for?

Seminole County has 697 registered brokerage firms, which works out at roughly 3.8 active agents per firm. That number flatters the market. Counting only firms that actually hold at least one agent, 61 percent of them hold exactly one, and the largest firm in the county holds about 4.6 percent of its agents.

So there is no dominant brokerage here. Two things follow. Most registered firms are a broker and a desk rather than an office with staff and a training calendar, and no firm's name on a business card is going to win a listing for you in this county.

How many new licensees is the county producing?

324 Seminole County licenses were issued for the first time in the twelve months to September 22, 2026, which is 7.6% of the active agent count.

Set that against the 38.9% inactive rate and the shape of the industry becomes visible: a steady stream of people entering at one end, and a considerably larger pool who have quietly stopped at the other.

What does an agent here actually earn?

There is no honest single figure, because agent income is a distribution rather than a salary, and the spread is enormous. What can be stated is the arithmetic.

A sale produces a commission. The commission is divided between the listing and buyer sides by agreement. Your brokerage takes its share of your side. What is left is gross income, before income tax, self-employment tax, the four recurring dues and fees, and every cost of running a business. You are self-employed, nothing is withheld, and roughly a third of what you see is not yours.

With Seminole medians running near $400,000, the variable that moves the outcome is transaction count rather than price. Two additional closings in a year changes the result far more than a better split does, which is why the question of where business comes from matters more than the commission percentage that recruiting conversations lead with.

How this compares with Volusia County next door

Worth setting side by side, because the two counties are adjacent and read very differently:

  • Active agents: Seminole 4,254, Volusia 4,816.
  • Inactive licenses: Seminole 2,711, Volusia 2,471.
  • Inactive rate: Seminole 38.9%, Volusia 33.9%, Florida 32.4%.
  • Registered firms: Seminole 697, Volusia 685.

Seminole has slightly fewer working agents than Volusia but more inactive licenses, in a smaller county with a stronger economy. That combination is the single most interesting thing in this data, and the most plausible reading is proximity to Orlando: a large, competitive metro market next door raises both the number of people who try this career and the number who find it harder than they expected.

How thinly is supervision spread?

Florida law places every sales associate license under exactly one active employing broker, and that broker carries legal responsibility for supervising the work. Seminole County's 1,058 active brokers against 4,254 working sales associates is roughly one broker for every 4.0.

That ratio is a crude instrument and it is still worth having, because it is the only public number that speaks to whether supervision in a market is likely to be real or nominal. A broker responsible for a handful of agents can review contracts. A broker responsible for many cannot, whatever the recruiting material says. When you interview, the question that gets at this is not how many agents the office has; it is how many that specific broker is responsible for, and who reviews a contract when they are away.

What it takes to enter this market

The entry requirements are set by the state rather than the county: eighteen or older, a high school diploma or equivalent, the 63-hour pre-license course, fingerprints and an application to the Department of Business and Professional Regulation, and the state exam. No college degree and no Florida residency requirement.

The step that is genuinely local is the one after: a license does nothing until an employing broker activates it, and in a county where 61 percent of firms holding agents hold exactly one, the range of what activation actually comes with is very wide. Then, before the first renewal, 45 hours of post-licensing education, and missing that deadline makes the license null and void rather than inactive, with no reactivation route at all.

Why we publish this

Florida releases the raw licensee file every week and essentially nobody counts it, so we do. These are not estimates or survey figures; they are counts from the state's own record, and anyone is welcome to use them with attribution.
