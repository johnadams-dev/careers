Seminole floods differently from coastal Florida, and slowly

Most of what is written about Florida flooding is about storm surge, which arrives with a hurricane and leaves with it. Seminole County is inland and its flooding does not work that way at all.

The risk here is riverine. The St. Johns River system forms the county's northern and eastern edge, and Lake Monroe, Lake Jesup and Lake Harney all feed into it, along with the Little Wekiva River on the western side. When heavy rain falls across central Florida, that water takes days to reach the river system and then weeks to leave it.

The practical consequence is the single most important thing on this page: a Seminole County property can flood from a storm that passed days earlier, under a clear sky. A buyer who watched a hurricane go by without incident and assumes the risk has passed does not understand what they are looking at.

What Hurricane Ian actually did here in 2022

This is not hypothetical and the figures are worth knowing precisely, because they are the clearest available illustration of how this county floods.

  • Over 1,200 homes were initially reported affected by flooding or damage, a figure that later rose to at least 2,000.
  • The St. Johns River at Sanford and Geneva stayed in flood stage for more than 56 days.
  • Over 120 homes remained flooded for more than 25 days, and some for the full 56.
  • More than 80 roadways were flooded for over 40 days. State Road 46 near the St. Johns River and Lake Harney sat under 10.8 feet of water.

Read those durations again, because the duration is the point. This is not a flood that recedes overnight and leaves a cleanup. It is water that sits in a house for weeks, and the insurance, repair and disclosure consequences are correspondingly larger.

Where the exposure is concentrated

Low-lying land subject to rising water in this county follows the water bodies: the St. Johns River, Lake Harney, Lake Jesup, Lake Monroe and the Little Wekiva River. In practice that means the northern and eastern edges of the county, including parts of Sanford and the Geneva area, carry exposure that Altamonte Springs and Casselberry largely do not.

That is a genuine difference between the markets on this site, and it is one an agent should know before showing rather than after a contract.

What the zone letters mean

Flood zones are mapped by the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the letters are not a rating of how bad a flood would be. They describe probability.

  • Zones A and AE are high-risk areas inside the mapped one percent annual chance floodplain, the thing commonly and misleadingly called the hundred year flood. A federally backed mortgage on a property here generally requires flood insurance.
  • Zone AE with a base flood elevation gives a specific elevation the water is expected to reach, which is what an elevation certificate is measured against and what drives the premium.
  • Zones X and shaded X are outside the high-risk area. Insurance is usually not required by a lender, which is a different statement from the property not flooding.

A low-risk zone does not mean no risk, and here that is not a technicality

Everywhere in Florida this caveat gets stated and largely ignored. In Seminole County it is load-bearing, because riverine flooding routinely reaches properties that sit outside the mapped high-risk zones when the river stays high for weeks rather than hours.

A buyer should hear the honest version: the zone determines whether a lender will require insurance, not whether water can arrive. Given what flood insurance costs relative to the loss it covers, the question is worth a buyer's own judgment rather than a lender's requirement.

How to find a property's real zone, and when

Do not rely on the listing, the seller's recollection or a previous insurance policy. Check the current FEMA flood map for the specific parcel, and check the county's own flood information, which maintains material on flood-prone areas in Seminole specifically.

Do it before the offer, not during inspection. A flood zone discovered late changes the insurance cost, which changes the monthly payment, which can change what the buyer qualifies for. Discovering that two weeks from closing is how a transaction dies.

Ask the seller directly whether the property has flooded and whether a claim has been made, because prior claims follow the property and affect what future coverage costs.

How this compares with the coast

Worth stating for anyone moving between markets. On the Volusia and Flagler coast the flood conversation is about surge, dune systems and wind, and the water arrives and leaves with the storm. Forty minutes inland in Seminole it is about a river system rising days later and staying up for weeks.

An agent who learned the coastal version and assumes it transfers will give a Seminole buyer confident advice that is wrong in both directions: too relaxed about a property near Lake Jesup, and too alarmed about one in Casselberry. The two counties carry 4,816 and 4,254 active agents respectively and plenty of people work across the line, so this is a common error rather than a hypothetical one.

What this does not mean

It does not mean avoiding waterfront or river-adjacent property, which is some of the most desirable land in the county and sells accordingly. It means knowing the exposure, pricing it honestly, insuring it properly and telling the buyer before they are committed. An agent who does that is worth considerably more than one who avoids the subject.
